# Positioning (April Dunford method)

A product without positioning is a directory of features hoping someone
guesses the value. This skill forces 5 decisions that, together, make
the product's meaning **obvious** to the right buyer in under 10 seconds.

## When to skip

For trivial copy edits or feature work inside an already-positioned
product, skip this. Use it when (a) launching something new,
(b) repositioning, or (c) a stakeholder can't finish the sentence
"this is the only X that Y for Z."

## The 5 steps (do in order — do not skip ahead)

### 1. List competitive alternatives

Not just direct competitors — **what would the buyer use if you didn't
exist?** This includes:

- Direct products (e.g., Yelp, Khoj Korun)
- Adjacent products (Google Maps + reviews)
- Manual workarounds (asking in a Facebook group, calling a friend)
- Status quo / doing nothing

Write these down. If you can't list 5, you haven't talked to enough
buyers.

### 2. Isolate unique attributes

For each alternative, what do you have that they don't? Be brutally
specific. "Better UX" is not an attribute — "verified by national ID +
phone OTP" is.

Cross out attributes you *claim* but can't prove. Keep only what is
**demonstrably true today**, not roadmap.

### 3. Map attributes to value

For each remaining attribute, ask: "so what?" Translate the feature into
the outcome a buyer cares about. Stop at the value level — don't drift
into emotional pitch yet.

| Attribute | Value |
|---|---|
| ID-verified reviewers | Reviews you can actually trust before spending money |
| Hyperlocal moderators in Sylhet | Spam removed within hours, not weeks |

### 4. Pick the best-fit market segment

Who cares about that value **the most, the soonest, and is willing to
act**? Look for "best-fit customer" signals:

- They have an acute, recurring version of the pain
- They have budget / agency to choose
- They are easy to find (concentrate, have a watering hole)
- They tell others (network effect)

Resist picking the biggest TAM. Pick the segment where you'll be
**obviously the best choice** — beachhead, not boil-the-ocean.

### 5. Pick the market category (frame of reference)

The category is the "compared to what?" filter. Wrong category =
wrong comparisons = wrong buyer expectations. Three options:

- **Win in an existing category** (you're a better X). Easy to explain,
  hard to differentiate.
- **Subcategory** (you're a new kind of X). Borrows category demand,
  carves a niche.
- **Create a new category** (you're not X at all). Expensive, slow,
  only works with a 10× story and budget to teach the market.

For most early-stage products: **subcategory** is the right answer.

## Output: the positioning statement

After the 5 steps, write ONE positioning statement using this template
(internal — not customer-facing copy):

```
For [best-fit segment] who [trigger / context],
[product] is a [market category]
that [key value, 1-2 attributes max]
unlike [primary alternative]
because [proof attribute that alternative cannot match].
```

Then derive 3 artifacts from it:

1. **One-liner** (customer-facing tagline, ≤12 words)
2. **30-second pitch** (problem → who → product → why-now → proof)
3. **Anti-positioning list** — who this is NOT for, what this does NOT
   do. Equally important; protects the segment focus.

## Common failure modes

- "For everyone who needs to find businesses" → segment too broad,
  product becomes generic
- Positioning a feature ("now with reviews!") instead of the product
- Borrowing language from the category leader → buyer just picks the
  leader
- Lying about attributes that aren't shipped yet → buyer churns
- Picking a category nobody searches for → no demand to capture
